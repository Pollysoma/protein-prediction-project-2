How we build the data

PP2 · embedding-based annotation transfer 9 November 2026 proposal for the supervisor meeting

What we are building, and why three sets

Our method is simple: take a protein, find the most similar protein in a labelled library, copy its label. To measure how well that works we need three sets.

SetWhat it is for
librarythe collection we search in. Every entry has a label.
calibrationquestions we practise on
testquestions we grade on, used once

Two sets would be enough if we only wanted an accuracy number. We want more than that. We want to be able to say “if the match is closer than X, you can trust it”. X is a number we fit from data. If we fit X on the test questions, the test stops being a test. So we fit on calibration and leave test alone until the very end.

Where the data comes from

CATH v4.4: a catalogue of protein pieces, called domains, sorted by shape into families. 123,251 domains in 6,630 families. The family is what we try to predict.

Two families used as examples throughout: 2.60.40.10 is huge, 10,133 domains. 1.10.8.130 has exactly two, 1gvnA00 and 2p5tA00.

The seven steps

Step 3 — why only one question per family

One per family stops the handful of huge families from dominating everything. Both papers we compare against do the same. The domain is picked at random with a fixed seed.

Step 4 — why we do not shuffle randomly

This is the step where a plain random split would be wrong. If we shuffled the 123,251 domains, near-identical twins would end up one in calibration and one in test, and we would be measuring memory instead of transfer. So we split whole families, and only then randomise inside that rule.

Step 5 — the 20 % deletion

If a near-copy of the question sits in the library, finding it is trivial and proves nothing: plain sequence search already solves that case in a second. 20 % is the standard line in this field, below which sequence comparison stops being reliable.

Two things worth knowing about this step. It runs once, before anything else, and produces one fixed library that every question searches. Nothing is filtered later, at search time. And an entry is deleted if it is too similar to any question, not only to yours — so most of what is missing from your library was removed because of somebody else’s question.

Step 6 — why some questions have to be thrown away

Step 5 can delete a family’s last remaining entry. Then the question has no possible right answer and has to go.

Being picked is not affected by size. Every eligible family gets exactly one ticket, whether it holds 2 domains or 10,000. The imbalance appears only here, at survival. What comes out the other side leans towards large families, which are also the easier ones, so we report results split by family size instead of as one average.

Expected size after this step: about 1,240 questions in calibration and about 1,240 in test — roughly 57 % of the 2,170 per side. The pilot drew 1,000 per side and kept 572 and 576.

The one real problem, and our fix

After step 5, 94–96 % of the remaining questions have no detectable sequence match in the library at all. That is not a bug. It follows directly from deleting every close relative, and it is exactly how the published benchmark we reproduced was built.

But it blocks two things we need:

  • The brief asks for accuracy grouped by how similar the question is to the library. Those groups would be empty.
  • A cutoff that gives 95 % reliability probably only exists among close matches. On an all-remote set the best model is right about 64 % of the time, so there may be no cutoff at all to find.

Decisions we have made

 DecisionWhy
AOne question per family, picked at random with a fixed seed Picking the longest or best-resolved domain would bias towards long or well-studied proteins. No paper defends either.
BDelete at 20 % similarity, 80 % coverage, using MMseqs2 Copied exactly from ProtTucker. ContrasTED and CATHe use the same.
CAccept that large families survive more often, and report results split by size Correcting it would trade one bias for another. Showing the split is honest and lets others compare.
DBuild both libraries, remote and full One gives comparability with the literature, the other gives what the brief asks for. Cheap, because the embeddings are shared.
EKeep CATH classes 4 and 6, but report them on their own line They are about 2 % of questions, so folding them into the headline adds noise. ContrasTED deletes them; we would rather keep and label them.
FOn the remote library, also apply the length-aware version of the 20 % rule A flat 20 % ignores length. One pilot question sat at 19.9 % similarity over 412 residues, which is still findable by sequence search. The length-aware rule catches that, and it is what ProtTucker used.
GDeclare the reliability targets in advance: 90, 95, 99 % Picking them after seeing results would be cherry-picking. We publish “not reachable” where no cutoff exists.

Tell us if you disagree with any of these. D and F are the two where your opinion helps us most, because they change what the benchmark measures rather than how it is built.

What happens after the meeting

  1. Freeze the input files. One day.
  2. Build and check the data, both libraries. Two days.
  3. Compute embeddings for all models, over the library and both question sets. One to two days on the GPU.
  4. Fit the cutoffs on calibration only, then freeze them.
  5. Run the test once.
  6. Then the same structure for EC numbers, which needs its own agreement because the labels are messier there.

Two traps we already hit

Both are handled in the script now, but worth stating so nobody repeats them.

  • A flat 20 % cut does not mean the pair is undetectable. Over 412 residues, 19.9 % similarity is still a real match.
  • MMseqs2 silently caps how many matches it reports per question. Raising that cap changed the library from 100,540 to 99,301 entries, so the first version of the filter was too weak.
